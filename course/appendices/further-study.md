What to read and listen to after the course, or alongside it, sorted by the
strand it serves. Everything here can be found by its title in a library or
a bookshop; the page gives no links. Each entry says what to look for and
which week it extends.

## The instrument

**The operation manual, read again.** After twelve weeks most of it will
read as confirmation. Reread the command list (pp. 69-75) and the
Macrosynth model descriptions (pp. 76-80) with your own bench-test results
beside them, and mark where the unit and the text disagree.

**The firmware changelog.** The manufacturer publishes one with each
firmware release. The course assumes 6.6.4; read the entries after it
for new commands and fixes, and check any module step that a later entry
touches.

**Your own results record.** The bench tests from weeks 1 to 11 form the
most reliable account of your unit that exists. Keep it, date it, and
re-run the timing tests after a firmware update.

## Sound

**The 1973 paper on FM synthesis in the Journal of the Audio Engineering
Society**, the one week 2 cites: "The Synthesis of Complex Audio Spectra by
Means of Frequency Modulation". A few pages, with the sideband arithmetic
of week 2 and patches for brass, woodwind and bells. Read it for the
reasoning from target spectrum to ratio and envelope.

**A textbook on the physics of musical instruments**, such as *The Physics
of Musical Instruments*. The chapters on bars, membranes and bells give the
mode ratios that weeks 2 and 4 use, and many more: marimba bars, cymbals,
gongs, timpani. Each table of modes is a target spectrum for an FM or
additive patch.

**A book on tuning and timbre**, such as *Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*.
It argues that consonance depends on the spectrum of the sound, and builds
scales to suit inharmonic timbres. With the M8's detuned scales (p. 28)
and your inharmonic bells, you can test the argument directly.

**A long series of magazine articles on synthesis from first principles**,
published under the title *Synth Secrets* in a British recording magazine
around the turn of the century. Several instalments synthesise drums,
cymbals, bells and pianos step by step, mostly on analogue instruments.
Translate each patch into M8 terms as an exercise.

**A general computer-music textbook**, such as *The Computer Music
Tutorial*, for additive, FM, granular and physical-modelling synthesis side
by side. Useful when you want to know which technique the Macrosynth models
borrow from.

**A book on procedural sound design**, such as *Designing Sound*, which
builds everyday and mechanical sounds from oscillators and noise. Its
analytical method (listen, describe the parts, model each) transfers
directly to drum and effect design on the M8.

## Rhythm and time

**A book on the mathematics of rhythm**, such as *The Geometry of Musical
Rhythm*. Euclidean rhythms, necklaces, timelines from many traditions:
every pattern in it fits a phrase or a groove, and it sharpens the
arithmetic of weeks 5 and 6.

**A study of polyphony and polyrhythm in Central African music**, such as
*African Polyphony and Polyrhythm*. It explains interlocking parts and
ostinato cycles of different lengths from a musical tradition built on
them. Week 6's phase tables and week 8's voice independence both grow
from here.

**A book on metre as perception**, such as *Hearing in Time*. It sets out
what a listener can and cannot hear as a metre, which helps decide when a
polymeter will read as such and when it will sound like disorder.

## Counterpoint, variation and form

**The classic species-counterpoint treatise of 1725**, best known in
English as *The Study of Counterpoint*. Short, dialogue-form, and the
source of the species exercises adapted in week 8. Work the two-voice
species on two tracks.

**A composition textbook built on the motive**, such as *Fundamentals of
Musical Composition*. It treats a piece as development of small motifs
through named operations, which is week 7's method and the reason for the
inventory.

**A theory of classical form**, such as *Classical Form*. It names the
functions of phrases and sections (presentation, continuation, cadence,
transition) more precisely than the usual textbook labels. Use its terms in
your one-page plans for week 10 and after.

## Listening

Listen with a pencil. For each piece, write down the cycle lengths you can
hear, where the material returns, and what changes when it does.

**Process and phasing.** The mid-1960s and early 1970s phasing pieces for
two pianos, for tape and for clapping by the American minimalists, in which
one part drifts or shifts step by step against another. Count the steps
between realignments, as in week 6. The large ensemble piece for eighteen
players by the composer of the two-piano phasing piece builds its form from pulsing chords and cues played on
a vibraphone: a model for week 10's process forms and for week 2's mallets.

**Tempo canons.** The studies for player piano by the composer who punched
his own piano rolls in Mexico City from the late 1940s. Several are canons
in which the voices run at different tempi in fixed ratios. The rolls are,
in effect, sequencer data: the nearest historical relative to writing
polyrhythm on a tracker.

**Piano études with crossed metres.** The late-twentieth-century cycle of
piano études by the Hungarian composer who championed the player-piano
studies above, especially those in which the two hands group the same pulse differently.
Weeks 5 and 6, written for ten fingers.

**Canons and inventions.** The two-part inventions, the canons at every
interval in the Goldberg Variations, and the puzzle canons of the Musical
Offering. Week 8's canon at the octave and the fifth come straight from
this repertoire; the inventions show how far one short subject can carry
sixteen bars and more.

**Isorhythm.** Fourteenth-century French motets and the mass setting by
the poet-composer of that period, in which a pitch cycle (the *color*) and
a rhythm cycle (the *talea*) of different lengths run against each other.
Week 6's isorhythm, six centuries early.

**Vibraphone and counterpoint in jazz.** The chamber-jazz quartet with
vibraphone, piano, bass and drums that recorded fugues and other
contrapuntal pieces in the 1950s. It shows a vibraphone carrying a line
inside a polyphonic texture.

**Electric pianos.** Late-1950s rhythm and blues on the reed electric
piano, and early-1970s jazz-funk on the tine electric piano, for the bark
of a hard-played reed and the bell of a tine. Compare each against your
week-3 patches at three velocities.

**Gamelan and carillon.** Balinese gong kebyar for interlocking parts and
for instruments tuned in pairs slightly apart so that they beat, the same
effect as week 2's ratio of 1.01. A carillon recital for the minor-third
tierce of a church bell, the 1.2 partial of the bell table.

**Tracker music.** The module music of the 1990s demoscene, made on
four-channel and eight-channel trackers under limits like the M8's one note
per track. Listen for how it fakes chords with fast arpeggios, as the `ARP`
command does, and how it makes long pieces from a small pool of patterns.

**Drum machines.** Records from the early 1980s built on analogue drum
machines, whose kicks and hats came from the same recipe as week 4's: a
swept sine, filtered noise and a few square-wave oscillators at unrelated
pitches. Compare their hats with the FM hat in the
[FM crib]({{ '/appendices/fm-crib/' | url }}).
