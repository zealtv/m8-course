## Overview

You have been using a form method since week 1 without having it argued. This week states it whole, adds the forms it can produce, and turns the Song view into a place to compare plans before you commit to one. The claim behind the week: a long piece is decided on paper, at the level of sections, curves and joins, and the unit is where you carry the decision out. The week runs to about eight hours, by agreement.

By the end of the week you can:

- write an inventory and choose which planning devices a piece needs;
- plan one inventory as three different forms, with durations worked from bars and tempo;
- draw density and register curves and say what each boundary changes;
- use tempo and key changes as structural events, and reset what they leave behind;
- compose a join by several means and choose between them by listening;
- audition section orders in live mode from proxy rows;
- realise a plan of at least 3 min 30 s in which nothing returns unchanged, and end it on purpose.

## Before you start

You need weeks 7, 8 and 9, and every plan you have written since week 1, with their listening-review annotations: they are this week's evidence. From week 1 you need bench test 1 (where a track loops to); from week 5, worked example 3 (the tempo arithmetic) and lab task 1 item c (which groove a track uses when playback starts mid-song).

Open a project that holds your baseline material and the instruments you would use for a long piece. Save a copy before you start, since this week moves song rows about.

## Concepts

### The method, with the evidence

The method has five habits (inventory, a plan on one page, named contrasts, transitions as objects, return with difference) and one rule, the repeat budget. Each etude since week 2 added a device to the one-page plan, usually without calling it one:

| Week | Bars | Device the plan gained | It answers |
|------|------|------------------------|------------|
| 1 | 64, planned only | inventory; section table; curves; boundary sentences | what is there, and where does it go |
| 2-3 | 24, 32 | written joins | how one section becomes the next |
| 4 | 48 | role per section | what a layer is for, and who takes over |
| 5 | 56 | division map | how each layer divides the bar |
| 6 | 64 | phase table and length ledger | when the layers meet |
| 7 | 80 | ordering principle for variations | why this comes after that |
| 8 | 96 | entry chart | who plays, rests and enters |
| 9 | 96 or more | voice-budget table | what each track is spent on |

A plan does not need all of them. It needs the section table, the curves and the boundary sentences always, and then whichever devices match what the piece is about: a piece driven by a polymeter needs its phase table, a contrapuntal one its entry chart.

Read your old plans beside their annotations and look for a pattern in your departures. Most people have one: sections that ran long, joins left until last, a middle that sagged, an ending that just stopped. The pattern tells you which part of this week's plan to spend time on.

Two working rules follow from eight weeks of evidence.

- **Skeleton first.** Lay out every song row of the piece with placeholder chains before you polish any of them. The loop habit is the habit of perfecting bars 1 to 4; a skeleton makes bar 80 exist on the first day.
- **Write the ending early.** Decide the last bar when you decide the first. A piece planned toward a known ending arrives; one that is not, stops.

> **Try it.** Lay your earlier plans side by side and write one line: the departure you made most often. Keep it in view for the rest of the week.

### Forms beyond the loop

A form is a rule for what returns, and when. Five that suit the instrument:

| Form | Scheme | Unity comes from | Plan must show |
|------|--------|------------------|----------------|
| Through-composed | A B C D E | one inventory, transformed; nothing returns whole | the thread: which motif or texture links each section to the next |
| Arch | A B C B′ A′ | returns in reverse order | the centre, and how each return differs |
| Rondo | A B A′ C A″ | a refrain | a different change for each return of A |
| Process, additive | one rule applied until it completes | the rule | the rule, its schedule, and what happens when it ends |
| Two-part | I, then II, unequal | one large contrast | the boundary, and whether II answers I or leaves it |

Some notes on each, in M8 terms.

**Through-composed** costs the most material. Week 7 supplies it: each section takes one item from the last and varies it by a named operation.

**Arch** is cheap in phrases, since B′ and A′ start as clones, and it carries a risk: the second half sounds like the first played backwards. Shorten the returns, change their texture, and put the piece's high point in C.

**Rondo** tests return with difference three times over. Plan the changes as a series: A′ re-instrumented, A″ in canon or over a new bass.

**Process** forms let arithmetic set the length. A phrase that gains a step each pass, its `HOP` moving from row `3` to row `F` (thirteen passes of 4, 5, ... 16 steps, by week 6's convention), lasts (4 + 16) × 13 / 2 = 130 steps, a little over eight bars. Week 6's realignments are process forms: the piece ends when the cycles meet. The danger lies in the listener hearing the rule early and waiting for the end, so pair a process with a second layer that does not obey it.

**Two-part** forms suit a change of tempo or metre at the boundary. The halves should differ in length, and the second usually carries more weight.

Proportion matters as much as scheme. Equal sections of eight or sixteen bars are a habit of the 4-bar chain. Give sections different lengths, and place the high point past the middle: a common choice sits about three fifths of the way through, which in 88 bars falls near bar 0.6 × 88 = 53.

> **Try it.** Name the form of your week-8 or week-9 piece from its plan. Then say what you would cut or move to turn it into one of the other four.

### Curves

A section table says what happens; a curve says how the piece is shaped. Draw two, one value per song row:

- **Density:** how many tracks sound, 0 to 8. For finer work, onsets per bar.
- **Register:** the lowest and highest sounding octave.

Written as a row of digits under the section letters, a curve fits in plain text:

```cycles
section   |A . . . |B . . . |C . . . . . |B'. . . |A'. . . |
density   |1 1 2 2 |3 3 4 4 |2 3 4 5 5 6 |4 4 3 3 |2 2 1 1 |
top oct.  |3 3 4 4 |4 4 5 5 |4 5 5 5 6 6 |5 5 4 4 |4 4 3 3 |
```

Four shapes cover most pieces: the ramp, the arch, the terrace (flat, then a step) and the sawtooth (build, drop, build higher). Check three things on any pair of curves.

- **Is there one highest point?** Two equal peaks compete.
- **Do the curves ever disagree?** If density and register always move together, every section change sounds like "more" or "less". A thin, high passage or a dense, low one widens the range of textures.
- **Does anything fall?** A curve that only rises has nowhere to go by the middle. The drop before a peak is what makes it one.

The voice-budget table from week 9 is the density curve with names attached; the entry chart from week 8 is the same thing per voice.

> **Try it.** Draw both curves for the last piece you finished, from the Song view, one digit per row. Mark the highest point. Was it where you meant it?

### Tempo and key as events

A change of tempo or key reaches every track at once, so it reads as a boundary of the largest kind. Use few of them.

**Tempo.** `TPO xx` sets the tempo from a phrase, and the help text converts the value (p. 72). Two uses:

- a step change at a boundary, by the ratio of two step lengths, so that a pulse carries across (week 5: the tempo scales by new step ÷ old step);
- a ramp over a join. Week 7's `REP` repeats a command with an added amount each step (p. 70); whether it will ramp `TPO`, check on your unit.

Every tick-counted time follows the tempo (envelopes, `DEL`, `RET`, grooves), so a tempo change rescales the sound of every instrument; week 5 gave the arithmetic. It also changes the duration sum. With sections at different tempos, add them one by one: bars × 240 / BPM seconds each.

**Key.** Three levels, from local to global:

| Means | Reaches | Cost | Page |
|-------|---------|------|------|
| chain TSP | one chain | a chain clone per transposed chain | 12 |
| `TSP xx` command, or project TRANSPOSE | the whole song | one command | 38, 72 |
| `SCG xy` | the song's key and scale | one command | 71 |

Instruments with TRANSP. off ignore all of these (p. 16), so drums stay put. `SCG` changes what the scale quantises (transposes, `ARP`, `PIT`; p. 28), so a change of mode can recolour a passage whose notes you never touch; what it does to notes already entered is among your week-9 open questions.

**Persistence.** `TPO`, the `TSP` command, `SCG` and `GRV` all leave their value in force after the phrase that set them. So a piece that changes any of them must set them again at the top: put the opening tempo, transpose, scale and grooves on row 0 of the first phrases, a **reset row**, or the second play-through starts in the final section's state. Starting playback in the middle of the song has the same trap in reverse.

> **Try it.** Put `TPO` with a slower value on the first row of a chain in the middle of an existing piece. Play through it, stop, and start from the top. What tempo do you get? Then add the reset.

### Joins and endings

Week 1 made a join an object: its own chain, cloned from the section's last and changed. This week you choose the means. A catalogue, each with what it needs:

| Means | What happens | On the M8 |
|-------|--------------|-----------|
| Fill | a layer breaks pattern in the last bar | a new last phrase on one track; `RET` rolls |
| Thinning | voices leave one at a time | empty phrases in successive tracks' last rows |
| Build | voices or notes accumulate | the reverse; a `REP` ramp on a level or a filter |
| Silence | a rest of a beat to a bar, then the downbeat | an empty phrase; write the `OFF`s or `KIL`s, or nothing stops |
| Pivot | one note or chord belongs to both sides | a held note with no retrigger across the boundary |
| Elision | the new section starts on the old one's last bar | shorten the last chain by a phrase |
| Metric modulation | a subdivision becomes the new beat | `TPO` and a groove change together |
| Arrival | cycles realign on the boundary | week 6's ledger |

State in the plan whether a join's bars belong to the section before it or stand between sections, or the bar count drifts. And vary the means: three fills in a row teach the listener to expect the fourth.

**Endings** are joins to silence, and they have types too:

- the **cadence**: a final event on a chosen beat, then nothing;
- the **thinning**: the opening run backwards, down to one voice;
- the **completion**: a process reaches its end, or cycles realign for the last time;
- the **cut**: full texture, then silence, where the stop itself is the gesture;
- the **tail**: a last sound left to ring through the delay and reverb.

Mechanically, a track that reaches an empty cell loops (p. 10), so an ending needs something to stop it. `HOP FF` stops its track (p. 70): put one on the row after the last note of each track's final phrase. For a render, set the last song row in the Render view (p. 47) and, if a tail matters, add a row of resting chains after the last notes so the effects have time to decay; whether a render runs on past the last row by itself, check.

> **Try it.** Put `HOP FF` on the last row of the final phrase of every track in a short song. Does the song stop, and does a held note stop with it or ring on?

### The Song view as a workshop

The song has 256 rows (p. 10), far more than a piece needs, and the manual suggests the rows below the song for trying things out. Four practices make use of that.

**Sketch area.** Keep the piece from row `00` and sketches from row `80`. An empty row between them ends each track's list, so playback from the top never reaches the sketches. A sketch block started with the cursor on its first row loops according to your bench test 1 result.

**Proxy rows and live mode.** Live mode ([SHIFT]+[LEFT] from Song view) cues tracks one at a time, and [LEFT]+[PLAY] cues a whole song row (pp. 10-11). A cued chain waits for the playing chain to finish unless LIVE QUANTIZE in Project view says otherwise (p. 38). So build one **proxy row** per section in the sketch area, each holding the section's most characteristic four bars, and you can play the sections in any order by cueing rows: an audition of the form's joins and proportions in rough, before any section is finished. A chain holds sixteen phrases (p. 12), so a proxy can stand for up to sixteen bars if you want proportions too.

**Bookmarks and row moves.** Chain bookmarks ([OPTION] three times, p. 11) mark section starts. Firmware 6.6.0 added row select ([LEFT] twice on track 1) with row moves and coloured row bookmarks, which let you reorder whole sections once a plan changes.

**Snapshots and copies.** A song snapshot can be taken with [SHIFT]+[OPTION] and recalled with [SHIFT]+[EDIT] in the Mixer or Project view (pp. 31, 39). The notes do not record what a snapshot holds, so test it on a copy before you trust it as an undo for row moves. When a plan is settled, CLEAR UNUSED frees what the sketches left behind (p. 38); run it on a copy first.

**Listening away.** The screen shows a few rows at a time; a render shows the piece. Holding [OPTION] in Song view with playback stopped gives the time at the cursor (p. 11), which converts a note such as "sags at 2:10" back to a row: bar = seconds × BPM / 240, so 130 s at 100 BPM lies 54.2 bars in, in bar 55, song row 54 div 4 = 13, `0D`.

> **Try it.** Enter live mode, cue a row with [LEFT]+[PLAY], and cue a different row while it plays. When does the change happen? Find LIVE QUANTIZE in Project view and note its options.

## Worked examples

### One inventory, two plans

The inventory:

| Heading | Items |
|---------|-------|
| Motifs | M1 bass ostinato, 1 bar; M2 mallet tune, 4 bars; M3 a 3-note answer |
| Grooves | G0 straight; G1 swing at 58% (`07 05`); G2 triplets |
| Instruments | bass, mallet, electric piano, pad, kit |
| Harmonic areas | H1 D dorian; H2 the same a fourth up (TSP `05`) |
| Textures | T1 bass alone; T2 tune over bass; T3 tune in canon; T4 full, with drums; T5 pad with fragments of M3 |

**Plan X: an arch at one tempo.** 100 BPM, 4-bar chains, a bar lasting 240 / 100 = 2.4 s.

| Section | Bars | Count | Song rows | Content | Density by row |
|---------|------|-------|-----------|---------|----------------|
| A | 1-16 | 16 | `00`-`03` | T1, then T2 in H1 | 1 1 2 2 |
| B | 17-32 | 16 | `04`-`07` | T4 in H2, G1 | 3 3 4 4 |
| C | 33-56 | 24 | `08`-`0D` | T5 growing into T3, then full; the high point at rows `0C`-`0D` | 2 3 4 5 5 6 |
| B′ | 57-72 | 16 | `0E`-`11` | B with the tune in the piano and the drums in G2 | 4 4 3 3 |
| A′ | 73-88 | 16 | `12`-`15` | T2 with M2 inverted, then T1; ends on a held D | 2 2 1 1 |

Check: 16 + 16 + 24 + 16 + 16 = 88 bars; 4 + 4 + 6 + 4 + 4 = 22 rows, `00` to `15` (21). Row *r* starts at bar 4*r* + 1: `08` at bar 33, `0E` (14) at bar 57, `12` (18) at bar 73. Duration: 88 × 2.4 = 211 s, 3 min 31 s. The high point, bars 49 to 56, starts 48 / 88 = 55% of the way through.

Returns: B′ changes timbre and rhythm; A′ changes pitch material and reverses A's order of textures.

**Plan Y: two parts joined by a metric modulation.**

| Part | Tempo | Bars | Seconds | Content |
|------|-------|------|---------|---------|
| I | 72 BPM | 36 | 36 × 240 / 72 = 120 | T1, T5, T2 in H1; slow, the piano in straight 16ths by the end |
| II | 96 BPM | 40 | 40 × 240 / 96 = 100 | T4 and T3 in H2; the piano's 16ths become the hats' triplets |

At the boundary the old 16th (6 ticks at 72 BPM) becomes the new triplet 8th (8 ticks), so the tempo scales by 8 / 6: 72 × 8 / 6 = 96. Check the pulse: 6 × 2.5 / 72 = 0.2083 s and 8 × 2.5 / 96 = 0.2083 s. Total: 120 + 100 = 220 s, 3 min 40 s, in 76 bars. Part II is shorter in time and longer in bars, and denser throughout.

The same eighteen inventory items give two pieces that share almost no large-scale behaviour. Plan X's interest lies in its returns; plan Y's in one boundary.

### Three joins for one boundary

Plan X, A into B at bar 17: T2 in H1 gives way to T4 in H2 with swing. Chain numbers give the track, then the row, so row `03` holds chains `13`, `23`, `33`. Each join is a clone of row `03`'s chains with the last phrase replaced, tried in the sketch area:

```song
# SONG   1  2  3  4      (1 bass, 2 mallet, 3 piano, 4 drums)
80       1A 2A FE 4A     ; join 1: fill and pivot
81       14 24 34 44     ; B's first row
82       .  .  .  .      ; empty: ends the block
83       1B 2B FE 4B     ; join 2: thinning and silence
84       14 24 34 44
85       .  .  .  .
86       1C 2C FE 4C     ; join 3: elision
87       14 24 34 44
```

| Join | Bar 16 | Dimensions changed at bar 17 | Risk |
|------|--------|------------------------------|------|
| 1, fill and pivot | the bass walks up D, E, F#, to land on G; a snare roll by `RET 04` on beat 4; the mallet holds its last A, which belongs to both areas | harmony, rhythm, density | the most ordinary; the roll announces the change a beat early |
| 2, thinning and silence | the mallet stops after beat 2 with an `OFF`; the bass plays beat 1 only; beats 3 and 4 are empty | the same three, after a gap | the gap may read as a mistake at this tempo: half a bar lasts 1.2 s |
| 3, elision | chain `1C` has three phrases, so B arrives a bar early, at bar 16, over the mallet's unfinished phrase | the same three, a bar sooner | A shrinks to 15 bars and every later bar number moves by one: redo the table |

Play each block from its first row, twice, then choose by ear and write down why. Join 3 shows what a plan is for: the choice changes the piece's arithmetic (87 bars, 87 × 2.4 = 209 s, a second short of the target), and the plan is where you find that out and add a bar elsewhere.

Two cautions. In join 1 the F# lies outside D dorian: under a scale, note entry gets quantised (p. 28), so enter it with the scale off or choose another approach note. And B's swing needs `GRV` on the first phrases of row `04`'s chains, with a reset to straight wherever A material returns.

## Lab

Allow 3 hours. Paper first; the unit from task 3.

1. **Inventory** (20 min). Take the baseline from week 1, or the material of any etude you want to go further with. List it under the five headings. Then add items until no heading has fewer than two, by week 7's operations for motifs and by weeks 5, 8 and 9 for grooves, textures and harmonic areas.
   *Product:* the inventory, with the added items marked.

2. **Three plans** (60 min). From that inventory, write three one-page plans in three different forms from this week's five, twenty minutes each, none shorter than 3 min 30 s. Each shows: the section table with bars, rows and the duration sum; density and register curves by row; a sentence per boundary naming the dimensions changed and the join's means; how each return differs; the ending's type; and the two or three devices from earlier weeks that the piece needs.
   *Product:* three plans, and one line on which you would rather hear.

3. **Proxy rows and live mode** (40 min). First the checks, on a copy of the project:

   - (a) Cue rows in live mode with LIVE QUANTIZE at each of its settings. When does a cued row take over?
   - (b) If tracks in a row hold chains of different lengths, do they change together?
   - (c) Take a song snapshot, move or change some rows, and recall it. What came back: rows, chains, phrases, mixer?

   Then build proxy rows from row `80` for two of your plans: one row per section, rough, three or four tracks. Play each plan's order through in live mode. Listen for the boundary that falls flat and the section that outstays its welcome; swap two sections and listen again.
   *Product:* results-record lines for (a) to (c); each audited plan annotated with what the audition showed.

4. **Three transitions** (50 min). Choose the most important boundary of your preferred plan. Compose three joins for it by three different means from the catalogue, each as cloned chains in a sketch block as in worked example 2. Play each twice. Choose one, and write two sentences: why it won, and what the losing two would have suited.
   *Product:* three sketch blocks kept in the project, and the choice with its reasons.

5. **Decide** (10 min). Choose the plan. Revise it for what tasks 3 and 4 showed, redo its bar and duration sums, and write the final bar in full: every track's last note and how each stops.
   *Product:* the plan you will realise, with its ending written out.

## Etude

**Brief.** Realise the plan as a piece sequenced straight through from the first row to a composed ending, in which no section returns unchanged.

**Forces.** Up to eight tracks, instruments you have built in the course.

**Constraints.**

- At least five sections, of at least three different lengths.
- Every return differs in a way the plan names. If the form is an arch or a rondo, each return differs differently.
- Each boundary changes two or three named dimensions. Every join has its own chains, and the piece uses at least three different means of joining.
- One tempo change or one change of key or scale as a structural event, with a reset row at the top of the song.
- Devices from at least two of weeks 5 to 9, named: a division map, a planned realignment, a labelled variation, a canon, a voice-budget table.
- One highest point, marked on the curves, later than the middle.
- A composed ending of a stated type, with every track stopped by `HOP FF` or by design.

**Duration target.** At least 3 min 30 s, 210 s. At one tempo that needs 210 × BPM / 240 bars, rounded up: 105 bars at 120 BPM, 88 at 100, 84 at 96. With tempo changes, sum the sections.

**Repeat budget.** 2, per track, counted as in week 7.

**Order of work.** About 3 h 30 min:

| Minutes | Task |
|---------|------|
| 30 | skeleton: every song row filled with placeholder chains, the reset row, the ending's `HOP FF`s |
| 100 | realise the sections in order of importance, not order of time: the high point first |
| 45 | the joins and the ending |
| 20 | a pass against the plan: budget, curves, bar sums, persistent commands |
| 15 | render, and check the duration |

**Plan.** The one from lab task 5, on one page.

**Hand in to yourself.** The project file, a render, the plan, the three sketch blocks, and three lines of reflection: where the piece departs from the plan, whether the departure is an improvement, and whether the departure you named in this week's first Try it showed up again.

## Listening review

Render the piece. The next day, listen once, away from the unit, without stopping, noting times, not bars. Then convert the times to bars and annotate the plan in a different colour. Thirty minutes in all.

1. Where did your attention drop? Give the time, convert it to a bar, and name what was happening on the curves.
2. Did the high point arrive where the plan put it, and did you know it when it came?
3. Which join did you not notice, and which one stuck out? Were those the ones you expected?
4. Did the ending sound decided? Would the piece survive losing its last section?

## Rubric

| Criterion | 2 | 4 |
|-----------|---|---|
| Plan and realisation | The plan lacks curves, boundary sentences or a duration sum, or the piece departs from it without comment. | The plan is complete on one page, its sums check, and every departure is annotated with a reason. |
| Technique of the week | Sections follow one another, but joins are of one kind, or the tempo or key change leaves stale state on a second play. | A recognisable form; at least three means of joining; the structural change is reset; the ending is of a stated type and stops cleanly. |
| Development | A section returns unchanged, or the curves are flat, or a phrase exceeds the budget of 2. | Nothing returns unchanged; one highest point past the middle; the budget holds across at least 3 min 30 s. |
| Sound | Dense sections blur and thin ones sound empty. | Each texture in the inventory reads clearly, and changes of density sound like decisions. |

## Summary

- A plan needs sections, curves and boundary sentences always, and other devices only where the piece calls for them.
- A form is a rule for what returns: nothing, in reverse, as a refrain, by a process, or across one large boundary.
- Curves need one highest point, a fall before it, and moments where density and register disagree.
- Tempo and key changes are the largest boundaries; whatever persists must be reset at the top.
- Joins and endings have types; compose several, in the sketch area, and choose by ear.
- Proxy rows in live mode let you hear a form before you build it. Skeleton first, ending early.
